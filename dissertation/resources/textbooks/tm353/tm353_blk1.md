1.1 The importance of success and failure in IT
systems
The module team has deliberately referred to ‘IT systems’ here rather than a
narrower term such as ‘software’, for two main reasons. First, most
technologies in use in organisations are a complex mixture of hardware,
software, networks and other infrastructure (such as data centres). Secondly,
the success of such technologies depends on factors that go well beyond the
purely technical, to include, especially, human and organisational factors, but
also many others. We’ll go into what we mean by IT systems in more detail
later in this Part. The term ‘information systems’ (IS) is often used in
academic literature, and some of the articles you will read later – and some
later Parts – refer to information systems where we talk about IT systems, but
to some people it now has an old-fashioned ﬂavour so we have chosen to
avoid it in this Part.

Let us state this again in rather black and white terms: this is not a module
about software development. When we talk about IT systems we mean both
more than technology and – within the technology – more than software.
Hardware technology is just as important for IT success as software
technology; and human and organisational issues are just as important as
technology. This is not to denigrate the importance of software, undoubtedly
one of the most important components of any IT system, and we will be
drawing on techniques and lessons from software development throughout the
module. However, since it is quite common in computing circles to equate
‘IT’ with ‘software’, we wanted to be clear up front that we’re not taking that
view in this module.

Another question which will be explored in more detail later is what is meant
by success and failure. The answer may often depend on the stakeholders

manager may consider a system successful if it comes to completion on time

a successful IT system is one that meets the needs (i.e. the goals or

needs of other key stakeholders related to, but external from, that

a failed IT system is one that does not meet the needs of an organisation
within which it is used and/or its other key stakeholders.

Success and failure of IT systems can be seen in many different settings.
They occur in private businesses, in the public sector and in the voluntary
sector. They can be found in very large or very small systems. The smallest
implementation of an IT system in a company can be very successful or very
problematic; the largest implementation can likewise go very well or very
badly. Large public sector failures get a lot of publicity because they are
spending public money and are subject to public audit; but private sector
failures are perhaps equally common, even though they are not widely
discussed. If a private sector project is a success, it is widely written about in
the computing press; if it is a failure it is often forgotten.

Note: Throughout this module we make considerable use of case studies.
Some of these are very recent (from the time the module was updated,
in 2023). Others are much older, sometimes going back to the start of
the twenty-ﬁrst century or even earlier. It is often just as useful to look
at older case studies as newer ones; although technologies (including
software and hardware) change rapidly, the way in which people and
organisations use those technologies changes a lot more slowly. In
addition, older cases – or ones which took place over a number of
years – have sometimes been more thoroughly analysed in academic and
IT trade publications than more recent cases.

On the next few pages, we’ll show you two short case studies: one of a

have equally been the other way around. After you read these case studies,
you will be asked to reﬂect on what makes them a success or failure, and
then to look for examples of your own.

Box 1.1: A successful IT system – pub chain Mitchells &
Butlers’ top-to-toe IT overhaul

Graeme Burton

Running a pub is a challenging business, with perishable goods to store
and sell, staff to roster to make the most of the British summer (if it
turns up) and, most important of all, treasured customers to serve in a
timely and friendly fashion. Running a pub chain, therefore, must be an
order of magnitude more challenging.

So the challenge of efﬁciently running the IT behind Mitchells &
Butlers (M&B), one of the UK’s largest pub companies with more than
17 different ‘brands’– as well as one in Germany – must be off the
scale.

Partly as a result of having grown by acquisition on the one hand, while
divesting itself of non-core businesses on the other, M&B had a
sprawling IT estate and, in some instances, inadequate bandwidth into a
number of sites.

‘We had a lot of legacy technology and therefore planned to go through
this “advancement of technology” process,’ says Martin Taylor, director
of business change and information technology at Mitchells & Butlers.

That is why, three years ago, the company decided to embark on a ﬁve-
year, top-to-bottom IT infrastructure refresh, taking in its data centre, its
systems and network topology, the systems it runs at its headquarters
and regional ofﬁces, retail systems, the level of bandwidth into
individual outlets – the whole lot.

At the heart of M&B’s new IT infrastructure is a private-cloud-based
architecture hosted by technology partner Fujitsu, which took over from
IBM as M&B’s primary technology partner in 2011, just pipping
Vodafone-owned Cable & Wireless to the contract.

In the process, IT at head ofﬁce was almost entirely virtualised, while a
new operational data store (ODS), based on Oracle, was installed to take
trading data from outlets on a just-in-time basis, radically improving
executive access to operational data. Everything connects via the ODS
and now head ofﬁce can see how individual units are trading on an
almost minute-by-minute basis.

The project is a radical departure on the old system, whereby much of
the company’s core IT was hosted on either IBM e Server p Series (RS/
6000) AIX Unix-based systems, or System I (AS/400) mid-range
servers, largely hosted in an IBM data centre. At head ofﬁce, the
computer room buzzed with some 20 different servers to handle email,
ﬁle and print, and some key business applications.

In most cases, existing applications – such as the all-important labour
scheduling and supply chain management applications – could be

have to use something slightly different for the odd brand, but in the

Box 1.2: A failed IT system – the BBC Digital Media
Initiative

Tara Conlan and Charles Arthur

The BBC has suspended its chief technology ofﬁcer and admitted
wasting nearly £100m on a ﬁve-year project intended to make the
corporation ‘tapeless’, saying that to continue with the project would be
‘throwing good money after bad’.

The Digital Media Initiative (DMI) was supposed to create a production
system linked to the BBC’s huge broadcasting archive, but ﬂaws in the
system peaked in April when instead of streamlining access to old video
footage, it caused chaos following Margaret Thatcher’s death.

Video editors were unable to access archive footage to use in news
reports via computers in New Broadcasting House in central London.
Instead they were forced to ferry tapes there by taxi and tube from the
archive storage facility in Perivale, north-west London.

In an email to all BBC staff on Friday, director-general Tony Hall said
he was halting DMI and admitted: ‘We have a responsibility to spend
licence-fee payers’ money as if it was our own and I’m sorry to say we
did not do that here.’

One insider called the DMI project ‘the axis of awful’, while another
source said: ‘The scale of the project was too big and it got out of
hand.’

John Linwood, the BBC’s chief technology ofﬁcer who was responsible
for the running of the project, has temporarily been replaced by BBC
News head of technology Peter Coles. Linwood was appointed in 2009
from Yahoo and had formerly worked at Microsoft. He receives a
£287,000 salary and was one of four of the organisation’s top managers
to receive a bonus last year – in his case amounting to £70,000.

BBC Trust member Anthony Fry said the project had ‘generated little or
no assets’ for the corporation. In a letter to Margaret Hodge MP, who
chairs the public accounts committee, which investigated the project, he
said: ‘This is because much of the software and hardware which has
been developed could only be used by the BBC if the project were
completed, a course of action which, due to technological difﬁculties
and changes to business needs, would be, I fear, equivalent to throwing
good money after bad.’

DMI has cost £98.4m, and was meant to bring £95.4m of beneﬁts to the
organisation by making all the corporation’s raw and edited video
footage available to staff for re-editing and output. In 2007, when the
project was conceived, making a single TV programme could require
70 individual video-handling processes; DMI was meant to halve that.

1.2 The sociotechnical nature of IT systems
The word ‘system’ means many different things in different contexts. In a
computing and IT context, it’s often taken to mean a computer system. The
basic argument of this module, as exempliﬁed in the case studies above, is
that an IT system consists of much more than software, hardware or other
technologies – it is a mixture of technology, people and organisation.

There are many other uses of the term ‘system’ outside of computing and IT
– we talk of the ﬁnancial system, the political system, the health system,
biological systems, social systems and ecological systems. These may seem
like quite disconnected areas, but in each case they identify a set of parts that
are connected to each other in important ways to make a uniﬁed whole. The
discipline of systems thinking, on which many of the ideas in the module
rest, is concerned with understanding systems and their nature.

Note: The above paragraph is the ﬁrst time you have seen a term in
bold, indicating that you should enter it, with a deﬁnition, into your
glossary. The glossary was described in detail in the Module Guide.
Please do this now for the term ‘systems thinking’.

A short deﬁnition of a system, within the tradition of systems thinking that
has long been used at The Open University, is:

a set of components interconnected for a purpose

This is one of those deﬁnitions where almost every word has signiﬁcance!
The basic deﬁnition is usually expanded upon in the following way:

1 A system is an assembly of components connected together in an

organised way.

2 The components are affected by being in the system and are changed if

they leave it.

3 The assembly does something – carries out a task, fulﬁls a function.
4 The assembly as a whole has been identiﬁed by some observer who is

The last of these points is especially important. Although the loose use of
phrases such as the ﬁnancial system and the health system (and even IT
systems) might sound as if they are describing real entities, for the most part
the systems they describe should be regarded as constructs because the way
that the system is understood has been constructed (either consciously or
unconsciously) by a particular individual or group who are observing a
situation, and viewing aspects of it as a system. The perspective from which
the situation is viewed will shape what the viewer identiﬁes as the ‘system of
interest’. As you will see later, where the boundaries of a system are deﬁned
– what is considered to be part of the system, and what is considered to be
outside the system – can differ greatly according to who is deﬁning those
boundaries, the purpose they consider the system to have, and even the time
when they are considering the boundaries.

For a non-IT example, consider ‘the healthcare system’. What could be
included in this? Groups and organisations that might be considered part of
it: general practitioners, hospitals, healthcare administrators, insurance
companies, dentists, opticians, patients, pharmaceutical companies,
pharmacies, government regulators, ‘alternative’ practitioners such as
homeopaths and acupuncturists, gymnasiums … the list could go on. Which
of those are part of the healthcare system and which are not? For some
purposes, it would be appropriate to include some of the above list within the
boundary of the healthcare system, and to exclude others. Moreover, the
boundary would be drawn differently in different countries and probably by
those holding different political or ideological viewpoints. To take just one
example, someone working for the National Health Service in the United
Kingdom would deﬁne the health system quite differently from someone
working for one of the large health insurance companies in the United States
of America.

All of this might seem somewhat abstract and philosophical. Does this mean
there are no such things as real systems existing in the world? Yes and no.
This view does not suggest that hospitals and pharmaceutical companies are
unreal – rather it suggests that there are many different ways to group
together those real entities (in the form of systems), and in the process of
doing so we construct a particular model of reality at a particular time.

It is worth saying that there are many different approaches, both academic
and practical, which use the idea of a ‘system’ as a way of understanding the
world. Some of them would agree with the above deﬁnition, others would
not. However something like the above (and especially the points about the
observer, systems of interest, and systems as constructs) has become widely
accepted over the past few decades. Ramage and Shipp (2020) provide an

overview of systems traditions and the different approaches to systems
thinking.

Even within this module, we draw on a number of different approaches to
systems thinking. There are (at least) four forms of systems thinking in this
module:

. The Open University tradition of systems thinking, which makes

considerable use of systems diagrams. It is used at length in this Part and
the following Part. This approach derives ultimately from the ﬁeld of
systems engineering, which systematically creates large-scale systems, and
is strongly inﬂuenced by the soft system methodology of Peter Checkland
(see Checkland and Poulter, 2006).

. The approach of sociotechnical systems thinking, introduced in this Part
and discussed in greater depth later in the block, was heavily inﬂuenced
by the ﬁeld of general system theory. This approach arose from the social
psychology of organisations, which initially studied social factors and
subsequently incorporated the impact of technology; by contrast, many of
the approaches found in this module start with technological factors and
incorporate social factors into their analysis.

. Later in this block in particular, you will meet ideas from complexity
theory. These ideas again have different sources to the above two
approaches – ultimately they derive from cybernetics (the study of
feedback processes and communication), but have developed in the
natural sciences, especially biology.

. Finally, in Block 2 you will encounter models of systems taken from
control engineering, which will guide your study of reliability and
dependability (especially in hardware but also in other aspects of IT
systems).

Each of these four perspectives are forms of systems thinking, in that they all
look at whole systems and seek to understand the relationship between the
parts and the whole, but they use these ideas subtly differently. This is a
module about successful IT systems, not about systems thinking as such, and
so the ideas we use here are presented pragmatically, in ways that are
intended to be useful, rather than as a theoretical discussion of ideas around
systems.

Before looking at what systems thinking can tell us about the nature of IT
systems, we will introduce the concept of sociotechnical systems thinking.
This is a strand of systems thinking that dates back to the early 1950s and is
highly relevant to this module. It is framed around the idea that the social

and the technical aspects of a system are inextricably linked. You will learn
more about sociotechnical techniques (and the history of the idea) later in this
module, but at this stage it is important to know the basic concept. This
module has, at its foundation, the idea that all IT systems are sociotechnical
– they cannot be understood without a sense of the relationship between the
social aspects (organisational and people) and the technical aspects (hardware
and software) of the system.

One of the long-standing practitioners of sociotechnical systems thinking,
Ken Eason, has written of the usefulness of the ideas and its importance in
making sense of IT systems, as follows:

What is important to me about sociotechnical systems theory is that it
provides a way of understanding the complex way in which people at work
co-operate and use tools and technology to get their collective work done. It
helps us understand how the operational reality of working life achieves the
goals of the organisation. It does so by treating the collection of human and
technical resources in the organisation as a system producing work and
focuses on the interdependencies between the people in their respective
work roles and the technical artefacts they use to get the work done. Two
properties of this work system seem to me to be all-important. First, if you
change one part of the system (say bring in a new technical system) the
interdependencies mean there are knock-on effects that may be positive for
performance but equally may lead to dysfunction in the overall system. The
second point is that the work system is an open system; it is subject to
changes in its environment, to the inputs it takes in and to the market for its
outputs. A successful system is one that can adapt to the turbulence of the
outside world and it is the people in their work roles who do most of the
adapting. An important question is whether the technical systems they use
are capable of supporting this adaptation; if the technology is not ﬂexible it

There is one further aspect of IT systems that is useful to consider at this

technology of an IT system to be developed from scratch; most contemporary
IT systems are now developed using one or more of the following
approaches:

.

the customisation of tools and products created by third parties (i.e. not

These are all evolutionary approaches – in different ways, they take an
existing system, or parts of a system, and take it forward in an incremental
manner. They treat the life of IT systems as a ﬂuid and ﬂexible process,
which changes over time as the needs of the organisation and its technical
capabilities change.

This is a very different perspective from the classic model of software
development, or IT systems development, as it is often understood.
Traditionally, IT systems were considered to follow a standard life cycle.
These stages derive from the early importance of systems analysis and
systems engineering techniques within IT systems work (that is, techniques
drawn from the analysis and implementation of large-scale physical systems).
Lewis (1994) remarks that this was the result of:

a number of authors [arguing] that certain types of activity were logically
required by the nature of the development process itself … information-
systems, like plants and animals, had their own natural and inevitable life
cycle.

(Lewis, 1994, p. 69)

There are many versions of the life cycle (e.g. Structured Systems Analysis
and Design Method, usually known by its initials, SSADM; Jackson Systems
Development; and Yourdon Systems Method), each with different precise
methods and stages, but most follow a similar pattern. Avison and Fitzgerald
(2006) suggest that six stages can be identiﬁed as common to all life cycle
models:

1 feasibility study
2 system investigation
3 system analysis
4 system design
5 implementation
6 maintenance.

Life cycle models typically assume that one stage is followed by another,
with little scope for return to earlier stages, and as a result early versions of
the life cycle model were often referred to as the ‘waterfall’ model, because
of the way that control over the design would ‘ﬂow’, in one direction only
(Sommerville, 2010). This derives from a view of project management that
regards the various stages of a project as each being under the control of a

different person or group, who has to sign off that stage (approve it as
complete) before it can pass to the next stage.

Life cycle models can be a useful attempt to model and control the
development of a complex IT system with an even more complex
organisational setting. They can make the process easier to understand and
more manageable. The methodologies based on life cycle models, and the
techniques that support those methodologies, are well understood and tried in
a large number of different settings.

Indeed, and despite the life cycle model’s origins in systems engineering,
Lewis (1994, p. 75) writes that ‘the life cycle idea … can, if poorly used and
unaccompanied by an understanding of the basic principles of systems
thinking, actually promote non-systemic practice in [information systems]
work’.

The weakness of the ‘maintenance’ phase of the life cycle model is especially
relevant here. Maintenance can consume between 40% and 90% of an IT
system’s costs over its lifespan (Bennett, 1996; Dehaghani and
Hajrahimi, 2013). Yet in the classic life cycle models, this is considered
simply as the ﬁnal, and least important, stage of the IT system development
process.

This line of criticism is not a new one, and it has led to various modiﬁcations
of the life cycle model, such as Boehm’s (1988) spiral model, which retains
the idea of a set of ideal stages of development, but repeats those stages
several times over. Extending this concept of many stages of development has
been a series of approaches based around rapid prototyping – quickly
developing prototype versions of software which can be tested and presented
to potential users, then modiﬁed in the light of problems to produce another
version, which is in turn tested and presented to users. Most prominent within
software development, especially since the start of the twenty-ﬁrst century,
has been the introduction of agile methods (Highsmith and Cockburn, 2001;
Conboy et al., 2011) which are clearly evolutionary in their approach.

An evolutionary approach more realistically reﬂects the nature of IT systems
than does a life cycle model, and sits behind many of the ideas found in this
module.

As a result of your studies so far, you should be aware that technology is
about much more than ‘stuff’; it is about how the technical interacts with the
social and the intended and unintended consequences of such complex
relationships in particular contexts at speciﬁc times. In this section we will
brieﬂy examine four perspectives:

.

.

.

.

technological determinism
social construction of technology
techno-scepticism
techno-optimism.

Technological determinism, in its most extreme form, posits that
technological developments are the prime driving forces in history;
technological ‘progress’ is inevitable and shapes the social world.

The social construction of technology perspective can be seen as the polar
opposite of technological determinism. The focus is on how technical
knowledge and innovation is constructed (shaped) by social processes and
views the technical and the social as inextricably linked. In this view
technology is not seen as an inevitable and autonomous progressive
phenomenon; the key task is to understand why speciﬁc technologies emerge
when they do, who uses them and for what purposes.

Techno-optimism is related to the deterministic position because it views
technology in a positive light as a neutral, progressive force, and emphasises
the beneﬁts of technological innovation over the risks.

Techno-scepticism, as one might expect, is much less sanguine about the
impact of technological innovation, and questions the idea of technology as a
neutral, progressive force and is concerned about the ownership and control
of technologies.

Note that sociotechnical systems is not considered as one of these four
perspectives as it has already been considered in this Part (and is explored in
much greater depth in Block 2). However it also considers the relationship
between technological and social factors in particular contexts.

A good worker needs a good toolbox. Having introduced you to the concepts
of IT systems as sociotechnical systems, we will now introduce a set of tools
for making sense of IT systems. These tools will also be used at various
points later in the module. In places they may resemble diagrams used in the
software industry and taught in other OU modules on Computing and IT,
such as the Uniﬁed Modelling Language (UML) – but though there are
similarities there are also important differences.

As you have seen, understanding a part of the world in terms of a system
requires a sense of what is inside and what is outside the system. This is
achieved by the placing of a boundary around the system to differentiate
those components that are being regarded as inside the system and those that
are not. It is often helpful to do this through using a representation of the
system, such as a diagram. The choice of where to place the system boundary
is crucial as it will inﬂuence how we use our understanding of the system.
There is no right or wrong in the placing of a boundary – it depends on what
is useful to make sense of a part of the world. The placing of a system
boundary includes some possible elements, and excludes others.

This is a very important act, and so it matters a great deal who is entitled to
make that choice. For example, consider a system which represents those
entitled to vote. In most western countries, this now includes all adults over
the age of 18. However, until a few generations ago, it excluded women (and
still does in some countries around the world). Some include younger people,
others do not. Some exclude convicted criminals (either currently in prison or
previously released), others allow these people to vote. Who gets to decide
that system boundary? It’s been highly contested for many years, and
continues to be so. This is an example of the importance of power in shaping
our understanding of systems, an issue to which we will return to later in the
module.

The elements identiﬁed as being outside of the boundary are said to be in the
system’s environment. This is not the same as our general use of the word
‘environment’ in talking about the natural world. The environment of a
system comprises those components that inﬂuence the behaviour of the
system or are inﬂuenced by it. The components of the environment can also
exert a degree of control over the system but the environment cannot be
controlled by the system.

For most systems, referred to as open systems, the boundary is somewhat
porous: elements in the environment may have a signiﬁcant impact upon
elements within the system, and vice versa. In the quotation from
Eason (2008) referring to sociotechnical systems, he mentions the importance
of open systems in understanding sociotechnical systems.

hierarchy of systems that ﬁt inside each other from which you have to select
the system level at which you will work by exploring the most relevant ones.

This is important because if thinking in terms of systems is to be of any use
it must involve more than mentally grouping a number of elements together
and calling them a system. The whole point is that these elements are
interrelated for a purpose, so it is important to be able to grasp the ways in
which they characteristically combine and interact. An understanding of these
interrelationships, of how certain elements ‘hang together’, is likely to
provide a basis for deciding what to include in the system in the ﬁrst place.

One more important systems concept to mention here is the idea of
emergence, which is often summarised in the phrase that the whole is greater
than the sum of its parts. This can be understood in terms of the properties of
the system at its different levels. An example that is often used is a bicycle.
The individual components – wheels, frame, brakes, cables, etc. – have their
own properties. But when they are assembled together to make a bicycle, the
system as a whole has emergent properties, such as the ability to allow a
rider to use it as a form of transport.

1.3.1 Diagrams and models
The diagram in Figure 1.3 is an example of a simple systems diagram
(speciﬁcally, a systems map). The Open University has used diagrams in its
teaching of systems thinking for many years, and they are an especially
effective way of representing systems. A diagram is a simple form of a
model, and like all models it both reveals and conceals aspects about the
world. Donella Meadows, an expert in modelling using a technique called
system dynamics (who among other projects once created a model of the
entire global ecosystem), argued that:

Everything we think we know about the world is a model. Every word and
every language is a model. All maps and statistics, books and databases,
equations and computer programs are models. So are the ways I picture the
world in my head – my mental models. None of these is or ever will be the
real world.

(Meadows, 2009, p. 86)

Models represent a partial view of a situation, allowing the user to gain
insight into part of it by representing it differently. They do not represent the
whole of a situation – they conceal as well as reveal. In particular, systems
diagrams are drawn by a particular person (or group), at a particular time,
and for a particular purpose – if drawn by other people, at another time or for
another purpose, they could look quite different. This might be a surprise if
you consider diagrams to exist principally as a way of illustrating text. In
fact, these kinds of system diagrams help to explain and analyse ideas. They
are slightly different in this respect from a numerical graph (e.g. a bar chart)
as the analysis work occurs through the process of constructing the diagram.
With a graph, the analysis work has already been carried out, usually through
a software package, such as a spreadsheet or statistical program, and the
graph is an output from that package.

Diagrams also serve an important role in enabling a group of people to
consider a situation together. Systems diagrams are sufﬁciently simple that
most people can draw them, given a few basic rules – they don’t need artistic
skills but rather an ability to analyse and document that analysis. This
simplicity distinguishes systems diagrams from techniques such as computer
modelling, which usually require considerable expertise in order to produce
the model. There are many examples of groups of people working together
using these kinds of diagrams to model systems. For example Simon Bell, the
author of some of the material in Block 2, has used diagrams extensively to
enable local groups to develop indicators of sustainable development in water
management issues across the Mediterranean basin (see e.g. Bell and
Morse, 2013).

Inﬂuence diagrams
Systems maps are very useful, but they only show the components of a
system, not the connections between those components. They can be
extended, however, by the addition of lines connecting the component parts
of systems, which indicate the inﬂuences from one component to another.
Inﬂuence diagrams are also used to show the connections between
components within the system and those in the system’s environment.

An inﬂuence diagram will therefore look like a series of blobs interconnected
by lines. Some of the lines sit within the main system boundary (for
inﬂuences within the system); others cross the boundary (to denote inﬂuences

or ‘has an effect on’, or‘ contributes to’ – although the strength of the

Feedback
Before we look at the third diagram type, multiple cause diagrams, we will
discuss the concept of feedback.

What does the word ‘feedback’ mean to you? In everyday use, it usually has
one of two meanings: either the irritating screeches you sometimes get with
audio equipment when a microphone gets too near a loudspeaker; or, in a
work context, giving comments to somebody on how well they’re doing or
passing on important messages from a meeting. But ‘feedback’ has a much
broader meaning – the way in which the behaviour of a system inﬂuences
(feeds back into) the future behaviour of a system.

The basic idea of feedback is straightforward. In some systems, the behaviour
is quite simple – there is an input, followed by some action that is made on
that input, and an output from that action. If, however, the output is fed back
into the input, then future behaviour will be inﬂuenced to some extent by past
behaviour. This pattern can be seen in a wide variety of situations. Your body

Part 1 – Successful IT systems

35

Part 1 – Successful IT systems

36

is full of feedback mechanisms to regulate temperature, guard against disease,
move your muscles, and so on. Decision making, whether it is individual, or
organisational change based on past actions, is usually based on feedback.
Temperature controls and thermostats of all varieties are based on feedback
mechanisms. There are many ecological examples, such as the balance of
population numbers between predators and prey (these will be studied in
greater depth later in Block 1).

Feedback comes in two varieties. Many of the examples we have just given
are about regulation and control of the system, where information about the
outcome of the process is being fed back to the beginning of the process in
order to control it. This is called balancing (or negative) feedback because it
leads to a dampening down or check of the effect caused by the input. There
is another sort of feedback, which can make systems run out of control. In
these cases, information on the outcome of the process is being fed back, but
instead of compensating and balancing, it exaggerates and reinforces that
process. This is called reinforcing (or positive) feedback because it acts to
reinforce the effect.

It is worth observing here that the terms ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ can be
rather misleading. They refer to quite a technical issue – the way in which
the variables change relative to each other through successive cycles of the
feedback loop. However, they might suggest that negative feedback is bad
and positive feedback is good. As the examples above show, this is not
necessarily the case, and it is for this reason that system dynamics, and some
other systems traditions, prefer the terms balancing and reinforcing feedback.

It is also the reason why we sometimes talk about virtuous circles and
vicious circles (for desirable and undesirable forms of reinforcing/positive
feedback). We shall use the terms balancing and reinforcing here; but you
may see the terms negative and positive feedback elsewhere.

‘The 1914–18 war in Europe started because of the assassination of Archduke
Franz Ferdinand of Austria in Sarajevo, on 28th June 1914’. You may well
have learnt this at school. Alternatively, you may have been taught that this
war started because of an interlocking series of causes, including (among
others) military alliances between the European powers, an arms race
between Britain and Germany, a number of past conﬂicts in the Balkans, and
the decline of the Austrian and Ottoman empires. Most historians would
subscribe to some version of the latter view (although they may not agree
with some of the causes, and almost certainly would add others). The
distinction between the two explanations is between single causality and
multiple causality: the ideas that events must have a single cause, and the
idea that very often they have multiple causes. Systems thinkers, keen on
broad explanations and wishing to avoid reducing explanations to a single
factor, generally favour multiple causality.

1.4.1 What is a successful system?
A succinct deﬁnition of a successful system is a system that:

achieved what was intended of it; it was operational at the time and cost
that was planned; the project team and the users are pleased with the result
and they continue to be satisﬁed afterwards.

(Fortune and Peters, 2005, p. 13)

On the face of it, this deﬁnition sounds straightforward, but once one starts to
examine it carefully one begins to see that it is not as clear cut as it at ﬁrst
appears. Let us look at it phrase by phrase.

achieved what was intended of it

This implies that the requirements for the system were identiﬁed accurately
and translated into stated and agreed objectives before the system was
developed and that it is possible to measure performance of the system
against these objectives in order to check they have been met.

… it was operational at the time and cost that was planned

Here it is assumed that there was an agreed and approved development plan
that included timescales and costs and that performance against this plan can
be measured. It also assumes that it is clear what ‘operational’ means and it
is possible to be certain when it has been achieved.

An important difference between these ﬁrst two parts of the deﬁnition is that
the ﬁrst one is about the success of the system and the second one is
concerned with the success of the project that built the system. For an
individual project, judgments of success are not necessarily the same across
both aspects. A project regarded as well-managed may fail to deliver the
intended outcomes whilst a project that experiences many problems can still
be capable of delivering success, though almost always at a price. There does

tend to be a positive correlation between the quality of the project
management and the quality of the output but there are exceptions.

… the project team and the users are pleased with the result

This is where the deﬁnition becomes even murkier with a number of
potentially conﬂicting judgements. First, there is the relative importance of
the views of the project team and the users. Some would regard the views of
the professionals who make up a project team as paramount but others would
say that the views of the project team are largely irrelevant as long as the
users are content. The next big question it raises is‘ who are the users?’ Are
the users just the people who interact directly with the system or do they
include those who may not know it exists but are users of the services it
supports? Are all users’ views equally important, and what about the views of
the client or customer? This part of the deﬁnition also implies that ‘pleasure’
can be measured or assessed.

… and they continue to be satisﬁed afterwards.

This shares the problems of the previous phrase. Who are the users whose
degree of satisfaction should be considered later? Another question is how
long a period of time is covered by ‘afterwards’. They may not be the
original users, however deﬁned. Measuring and/or assessing satisfaction is
also an issue here. Should the same criteria be used as when the system was
new or might there be a different set of standards now that users are familiar
with the system? Another issue is what allowances should be made for the
passage of time. For example, the environment may have changed to such an
extent that there is now a need for the system to do things that were not
envisaged when the system was designed.

Hopefully you can see from the examination of this deﬁnition that complexity
and ambiguity surround the notion of success and, indeed, failure.

1.6 Power and success in IT systems
We have looked at the nature of success in IT systems, and the different ways
in which that success is understood by different stakeholders. The previous
section touched brieﬂy on the very important concept of power, which
governs the relationships between the stakeholders of an IT system.

Power manifests itself in various ways in different settings including decision
making, agenda setting and in the shaping of felt needs: think about the
power of media such as newspapers, television and social networks such as
Facebook, YouTube and Twitter in shaping public opinion on a range of
issues. But what exactly is power?

Notwithstanding the fact that as Clegg (1989, p. xv) has argued ‘there is no
such thing as a single all-embracing concept of power per se’, it is important
to get to grips with this notion. We might begin by deﬁning power as the
ability or capacity to perform or act effectively; alternatively, we might deﬁne
it as the ability to direct or inﬂuence the behaviour of others or the course of
events in the pursuit of some goal or agenda. In his classic study, Power: A

54

Radical View, philosopher Steven Lukes (1974) identiﬁes the most basic
concept of power as follows:

A exercises power over B when A affects B in a manner contrary to B’s
interests.

(Lukes, 1974, p. 37)

Importantly, on this view, power is something that is necessarily antagonistic
or ‘oppositional’, only operating at sites of conﬂict between individuals and
groups. What this means is that in the absence of conﬂict – in more technical
language, where power remains unopposed or ‘uncontested’ – there simply is
no exercise of power. (In Block 2, you will brieﬂy explore an alternative
view where power can be wielded in the absence of conﬂict.)

Yet another way of thinking about the issue is presented by Clegg (1989,
p. xv) who identiﬁes three family groupings of concepts of power:

1 Dispositional – power as a set of abilities.
2 Agency-based – power as the ability to bring about events or cause

changes to occur.

3 Facilitative – power as the ability to achieve goals, that is, to get things

done.

In the ﬁrst of these groupings, power is understood as something that can be
possessed by an individual or group. By contrast, in the other two groupings,
power is understood as something that is necessarily ‘situational’ in nature,
that is, determined relative to a‘ background’ context constituted by a
network of relations between various actors – for example, stakeholders – in
an organisational setting. Another important distinction is that between the
power of agents (individuals, groups, organisations, etc.) and the power of
structures in which such agents are embedded. Structural power can manifest
in different domains – social, economic, cultural, technological and so on–
and take a variety of forms including legal, ﬁnancial, governmental and
educational institutions.

Issues of power are central when considering the role played by different
stakeholders in planning IT systems and, as you saw at the end of the last
section, stakeholders can enhance their power by coming together, mobilising
their power in pursuit of a common goal or agenda. According to Clegg
(1990) and others, the process of mobilising power is what is known as
politics, and in the remainder of this section our focus will be on issues of
politics and stakeholder power relations within organisations.

1.6.1 Politics and rationality
The original meaning and idea of politics comes from ancient Athens, where
it stood for a means by which society allowed individuals to reconcile their
differences through consultation and negotiation. Politics is usually discussed
in relation to issues of government; however, insofar as organisations (or
enterprises) are comprised of collectives of people, it is possible to view
organisations as systems of government, and therefore as political entities.

Recognising organisations as political systems is important because it exposes
what has been referred to as ‘the myth of organisational rationality’ – the
view that all the members of an organisation are committed to pursuing
common goals which are objective, neutral and rational, that is, value-free. In
reality, organisational goals may be rational for some people (e.g. senior
management, board of directors) but not for others (e.g. IT personnel). This
means that rationality is always interest-based and dependent on the
perspective from which it is viewed; in short, rationality is always political.
In terms of IT systems, this means that planning, designing and building
these processes, the people involved in them and the many activities and
mechanisms they promote are by no means neutral as they may initially
seem.

Organisational politics tends to go unnoticed as long as the interplay of
competing interests within an organisation remains relatively ordered such
that there is an absence of visible conﬂict. This means that when conﬂict
does start to emerge, it appears that it is politics that is the cause of the
trouble, whereas in reality politics is merely the mechanism through which
power is exercised in pursuit or defence of interests. It is important to
appreciate that the structure and culture of many organisations tends to
encourage conﬂict by simultaneously requiring people to compete for
resources, status and remuneration and collaborate within and between teams,
departments, organisations and even countries.

convention, etc. are also at work. However, formal and informal systems of

gap’ –a d ifference between the planned and actual outcome of a project,
e.g. technologies being designed to do one thing but when implemented being
used for another (or functions being ignored).

In mainstream views, mechanisms of domination such as leadership, culture
and structure are usually treated as neutral, inevitable, or objective, and hence
unproblematic.

The separation of formulation from implementation, downplaying the
importance of the relationship between formal and informal systems of
control and assumptions about the compatibility of organisational and
individual/group goals is likely to encourage ignorance of the possibility of
‘goal incongruence’ (or mismatch of stakeholder objectives), and therefore of
the possibility of conﬂict between stakeholders. This situation is further
aggravated because conventional approaches to management encourage the
belief that those involved in managing the implementation process act as
rational and neutral actors when carrying out their responsibilities and
actions. Consequently, power and interests remain invisible until the point at
which conﬂict occurs when power and politics become a necessary resource
of management to defeat conﬂict.

1.6.5 Expert stakeholder power
In addition to the role played by management in inﬂuencing the approach to
IT system implementation in an organisation, it is important to consider how
power is exercised by experts such as designers and programmers. Experts
are able to exercise power through the control of access to and accumulation
of specialist knowledge and information about technology. They wield
signiﬁcant power in many situations, including system design and
implementation, because they possess knowledge and skills that others do
not. In addition, experts who belong to professions are often able to limit
access to the power loop associated with system implementation shown in

Some professions are more powerful than others, and over time the power of

particular, and stakeholders in general (though less effectively), try to control

words, the power and/or inﬂuence that an individual or group has today
might not be the same as they had last week or will have in the future.

Finally, it is worth noting that the power of experts and stakeholders is very
likely to differ at different stages of the system life cycle in the case of
systems developed using the ‘waterfall’ approach described in section 1.2.1;
for systems developed using evolutionary approaches the situation is more
complex with reversals in power relations occurring due to the presence of
feedback loops between stakeholders and incremental adaptation which
sometimes proceeds very rapidly. Stakeholder power can also change with
adoption of an approach to systems development incorporating a particular
set of values. For example, in a study conducted by McAvoy and Butler
(2009) looking at the introduction of agile methods in a software
development team, it was discovered that the power associated with the
desire for cohesion and conformity within the team which required a ‘ﬂatter’,
more symmetric distribution of power between the stakeholders led to
ineffective decision-making and learning. While recognising that such
outcomes might be speciﬁc to particular deployments of such methods, it is
important to appreciate that promoting different values and pursuing different
goals results in different conﬁgurations of power; furthermore, that
determining which values end up being promoted and which goals end up
being pursued depends on power relations between stakeholders.

In thinking about IT systems development and operation, two things are
incontrovertible. The ﬁrst is that some failures will always occur, and the
second is that many of those that do occur are avoidable. Many studies have
been carried out to try toﬁ nd why the introduction and effective operation of
IT systems are so problematical. They have shown that the same sorts of
things are going wrong again and again. The reasons why problems are not
avoided are manifold, but a major one is the inability of people to learn from
mistakes. This Part of Block 1 promotes such learning by providing a general
understanding of the nature of IT system failure and a systems methodology
by which it can be analysed, understood and predicted.

The main argument that will be made is that through the use of
sociotechnical systems thinking it is possible to gain insights into failure that
would not otherwise be available. The purpose is to provide you with a
means of learning from failure that you can incorporate into your own
practice as an informed user, a commissioner or a developer of information
systems with a view to preventing failures in design, implementation,
modiﬁcation and operation. This ‘means of learning’ is called the Systems
Failures Approach.

As you will see, the Approach concentrates on analysing an IT system in the
context in which it is being used to develop understanding of how and why
the system has failed in one or more respect. Often that will mean looking at
an existing system, or even one that failed to materialise fully or was
abandoned, but the Approach can also be used to predict whether new
systems are likely to succeed or fail.

In many ﬁelds practitioners have long recognised learning from failure as a
crucial element in their development. Jung, the founder of analytical
psychology, described the process thus:

The psychotherapist learns little or nothing from his successes. They mainly
conﬁrm him in his mistakes, while his failures, on the other hand, are
priceless experiences in that they not only open up the way to a deeper
truth, but force him to change his views and methods.

(Jung, 2001, p. 59)

Ackoff (1994), one of the world’s leading business thinkers and one of the
founding fathers of systems thinking, made a similar point:

When one does something right, one only conﬁrms what is already known:
how to do it. A mistake is an indicator of a gap in one’s knowledge.
Learning takes place when a mistake is identiﬁed, its producers are
identiﬁed and it is corrected.

(Ackoff, 1994, p. 3)

The quotations from Jung and Ackoff are both about individuals learning
from their own experiences, but learning from failure can be extended, as it
will be here, beyond direct personal experience so that it encompasses
learning from situations in which one played no part, and of which one might
have had no direct experience. Used in this way, systems analysis of failures,
by giving you opportunity to analyse and reﬂect, and thus gain insight, allows
you to achieve this learning.

2.2.1 Rich pictures
Rich pictures were developed as part of Soft Systems Methodology, which
you will encounter in Block 2. They are used for gathering information about
a complex situation (Checkland, 1981), and comprise a compilation of
drawings, pictures, symbols and text that represent a particular situation or
issue from the viewpoint(s) of the person (or people) who drew them. They

2.2.2 Spray diagrams
Spray diagrams show the connections between related elements or concepts
associated with a particular issue. They are closely related to mind maps and
are a frequently used diagramming technique in many disciplines and ﬁelds
of work. They are widely employed as thinking tools in contexts such as :

.

.

.

.

exploring an issue from scratch
helping you to organise various facts, issues or ideas into a structured
form
taking notes
setting out the structure of an argument.

You can use a spray diagram to organise material into a basic structure, both
to clarify your own understanding and/or as the basis for a report or
presentation. The diagram you create can therefore be used as a tool to give
an overview of your understanding of a situation, and to enable you to share
that understanding in discussions with others.

The form of a spray diagram is very simple: it consists of lines, a few blobs
and words at the ends of lines or where they branch. They generally do not
show the nature of the relationship between the elements represented, so
there are no arrows, and they typically do not include images or coloured

lines (which is how they differ from mind maps, which often do include
these items).

It’s a good idea to circle the topic or central idea that the diagram sprays out
from to give a visual indicator of what the central idea is. Some people also
ﬁnd it useful to circle other sub-topics on the diagram. You don’t have to
think about the nature of the connection between two nodes joined by a line;
the line simply means that, in your mind, there is some association. Do not
show directional links on the lines.

2.3.2 Systems maps
You have already met system maps in Block 1 Part 1, and you may wish to
refresh your memory of the descriptions and animations in that Part. (The
animations can be found in the Block 1 Part 1 Resources section of the
module website.) There is information about these diagrams on pages 107 of

The list below provides further guidelines on drawing systems maps:

1 It should be clear which is ‘the’ system boundary. The system boundary
can be emphasised by colour, or by a thicker line. A dashed line can be
used to emphasise that the boundary is subjective and tentative.

2 Irregular blobs are normally preferable to regular boxes. Boxes imply that
systems or subsystems are clearly deﬁned, which is seldom the case.
Boxes also have the practical disadvantage that the eye ﬁnds it hard to
distinguish between a series of parallel lines.

3 Use overlaps sparingly. They tend to reduce the impact and clarity of the
map. Overlap only when the sharing of components is important from
your particular viewpoint. This applies equally to components within and
spanning the boundary. It is virtually impossible to interpret overlaps on
more than three subsystems, so avoid showing these at all costs.
4 Consistency between components is important. A systems map shows

structure so avoid representing system properties as elements.

5 Although the size of blob used is not determined by size, importance, or

other characteristics of the component that it represents, it makes sense to
show important subsystems at a reasonable size, and less important ones
somewhat smaller, as this is the way relative size is likely to be
interpreted by a reader.

6 Similarly, although there are no ﬁrm rules on positioning of components

(other than nesting one within another and overlapping) it makes sense to
put important components close together. This will facilitate subsequent
additions of subsystem boundaries.

7 It is a good idea to leave some space within your map. Not only does this

allow components to stand out clearly, but it leaves room for any
components you may wish to add later.

2.5 Summary and lessons for the future
So far we have been concerned with situations that have already been
labelled as failures, but in the ﬁnal section of this Part we shall be looking at
how the Approach can be used to try to prevent failure.

2.5.1 Preventing failure
The Approach can provide two types of assistance in preventing failure. The
ﬁrst is to generate lessons from past failures that can be used to avoid the
same mistakes being made again. The second is to look at systems that are in
the planning stage. For the latter, a proposed system is modelled and then
compared with the FSM to reveal weaknesses in the proposals and to identify
the subsystems and connections that need to exist if the system is to be
capable of being operated without failure.

2.5.2 Learning lessons from past failures
One way of learning lessons is to look at a set of previous attempts to build
and operate systems that are similar to the one being planned in order to
build an understanding of what has worked well and what has not. Clearly,
this can involve a lot of effort because it is necessary to identify and gather
data about those attempts as well as undertake the analyses. However,
providing suitable comparators can be found, an exercise like this can be very
worthwhile because it provides highly relevant lessons.

A shortcut is to draw on a list of ﬁndings assembled across a wide range of
failure studies that highlight the most common causes of failure and to

102

concentrate on preventing those. This does provide much less rigour but is far
better than simply repeating common errors. The list of recurring themes that
emerge from comparisons with the FSM is:

. Deﬁciencies in the structure of the system, such as a lack of a
performance-measuring subsystem or a control/decision-making
subsystem.

. No clear statements of purpose supplied in a comprehensible form to the

system from the wider system.

. Deﬁciencies in the performance of one or more subsystems – for

example the performance-measuring subsystem may not have performed
its task adequately.

. Lack of an effective means of communication between the various

subsystems.
Inadequate design of one or more subsystems.

. Not enough consideration given to the inﬂuence of the environment, and
insufﬁcient resources to cope with those environmental disturbances that
were foreseen.
Imbalances in resource allocation that lead to quality problems or to

transformations, and in some cases were isolated from the wider system.

2.5.3 Modelling the future
Essentially, modelling the future is not very different from looking at past
failures, but instead of investigating an existing situation you model the
system that might exist and compare that with the FSM. For example, what
will lie in the environment? In what ways might the environment try to
disturb the system? How can the system inﬂuence the environment effectively
(and so on)? Again, an example can be seen in Chapter 9 of the ebook (see
pages 179–185). A systems map was made of a proposed electronic patient
record system and comparisons made with the FSM at three different
hierarchical levels: the patient care system-level; a message system-level; and
a computer system-level. Taken together with the ﬁndings generated by
looking back at other systems, the comparisons enabled recommendations to
be made to those building the system on how to avoid failure.

Introduction
In the ﬁrst Part of this module, three kinds of failure were discussed in the
context of IT system implementation: inappropriate objectives being set for
the system; agreed objectives not being met; and undesirable side effects
being produced. In the second Part, you looked at some examples of real IT
systems that failed, with a view to examining the reasons for their failure in
detail.

Thus far, when considering issues related to IT system success and failure,
attention has focused on systems which are viewed in terms of systems
hierarchies – that is, composed of interconnected subsystems, each of which
can be viewed as a system in its own right. Viewing systems in this way
makes it possible to apply traditional engineering approaches based on
scientiﬁc reductionism which assume that a system can always be
reductively decomposed into a collection of simpler subsystems and the
relationships between them, which can then be further decomposed ultimately
into a set of irreducible components as shown in Figure 3.1 (overleaf).

Importantly, this reductionist approach results in a hierarchically organised
view of the system which tends to incorporate the idea of a single overall
executive controller (i.e. supervisor or ‘boss’ component) at the top of the
system hierarchy – in short, a top-down and centralised system model.
However, in the case of highly interconnected systems composed of tens of
thousands of components or nodes in a network, some systems thinkers such
as Bullock and Cliff (2004) have questioned the validity of this model. In
their view:

Having such a single point of overall control introduces some problems
which rapidly grow in severity as the number of nodes reporting up the
hierarchy increases: simply gathering and routing all the necessary data up
through the hierarchy can swamp the available network bandwidth and can
often introduce signiﬁcant delays. These delays, compounded by any noise
or uncertainty in the data, can lead to incorrect actions or commands being
issued by the master control node.

(Bullock and Cliff, 2004, p. 3)

with the complexity and networked nature of such systems. It should be
noted that at the time of writing, which is almost two decades since Bullock
and Cliff made these observations, the IT systems landscape has only
increased in complexity, with various phenomena (including the ongoing
rollout of the Internet of Things and increasing deployment of artiﬁcial
intelligence and machine learning technologies) contributing to such
complexiﬁcation. One such development is the expansion of cloud
computing. Internet commentator John Naughton (2012), drawing on earlier
work by John Hagel and John Seely Brown, argues that cloud computing has
undergone a four-stage progression: ‘(1) infrastructure as a service (i.e. raw
computing power and electronic services), … (2) platform as a service
(i.e. tools and environments to build and operate cloud applications and
services), … (3) software as a service, and … (4) business as a service’
(pp. 198–199).

build, there are still ways forward, including new ways of structuring and
approaching systems engineering, and organising and managing
sociotechnical systems of the complex, networked kind described above.

ﬁelds of complexity theory and complexity science, and we will look at some
key concepts associated with these ﬁelds in the next section. However, before

IT systems – from networks to complexity

Note: In this module, the term IT system should be understood as
broadly synonymous with the term information system (IS) which is
also in use.

One of the principal drivers for considering the application of ideas from
complexity science to IT systems is that IT, organisational and social systems
have been and continue to be increasingly networked together in ever larger
conﬁgurations. Over 15 years ago, Merali and McKelvey (2006) observed
that ‘the IS and the information technology (IT) landscape is characterized by
network dominance and increasing complexity’. In their view:

The network motif is a recursive one. First, the potency of discrete
advances in hardware and software capabilities to generate signiﬁcant
change in business and society is realized through the mobilization of
network effects. Second, technological advances escalate the potency of
network effects by continually enhancing the connectivity and bandwidth of
networks. Third, the growth of IT-enabled socio-economic networks is
accompanied by globalization and an increase in the number and
heterogeneity of players who can affect the dynamics of networks. Recent
work elucidating the relationship between network topologies and network
dynamics illustrates that the low cost of connectivity supported by the
internet means that emergent new network order is cheap. … The net effect
of this is a perception that individuals and organizations have to deal with a
world that is increasingly dynamical, complex and uncertain, and that their
actions may have unintended consequences that impact on other parts of the
world.

(Merali and McKelvey, 2006, p. 211)

In short, networks had become pervasive in the world of IT systems and this
trend has continued. It has also been complemented by additional
developments including the spread of cloud computing and, at the time of
writing, the increase in AI (more speciﬁcally, machine learning) technology.
For this reason, engaging with complexity science had, at the time that
Merali and McKelvey made their observations, become necessary since it
provided:

A source of concepts for enabling the trans-disciplinary exploration of
complex organization in the network economy and network society, and for
explaining the dynamics of networked systems at different levels of
description ranging from the micro- to the macro-level.

(Merali and McKelvey, 2006, pp. 211−212)

In this Part, you are going to explore how such ideas can – and should – be

However, before beginning to explore ideas associated with complexity, it is
important to clarify at the very outset how complex IT systems as a whole
are understood in this Part.

Following Naughton (2012) and others, we take the view that complex IT
systems (he refers to ‘media environments’) are usefully understood in
ecological terms. Brieﬂy, the science of ecology deals with how organisms
interact with each other and their environment, with a view to understanding

understood in its technical sense – the production of structures that can
survive and propagate for an arbitrarily long time, which has implications for
IT systems in terms of resilience and lifespan. As will be argued later in the

attempting to position them in Class IV so as to ensure robust and adaptive
behaviour in the face of changing environments.

Complexity theorist and artiﬁcial life researcher Christopher Langton (1989)

Linear systems are those which obey the superposition principle. We can
break up complicated linear systems into simpler constituent parts, and
analyse these parts independently. Once we have reached an understanding

This is not possible for non-linear systems, which do not obey the
superposition principle. Even if we could break such systems up into
simpler constituent parts, and even if we could reach a complete
understanding of the parts in isolation, we would not be able to combine
our understandings of the individual parts into an understanding of the
whole system. The key feature of non-linear systems is that their primary
behaviours of interest are properties of the interactions between parts, rather
than being properties of the parts themselves, and these interaction-based
properties necessarily disappear when the parts are studied independently.

(Langton, 1989, p. 41)

What this means for IT systems is that as they become more complex, and
heterogeneous (i.e. diverse), it will become increasingly difﬁcult to
understand and predict how they will behave. Non-linearity has profound
implications for IT systems since it undermines the possibility of applying
more conventional quality assurance methods and engineering techniques
associated with simple and complicated systems. This has led some theorists
such as Rasmussen and Barrett (1995) to argue that simulation is the only
possible means by which to model, understand and predict the behaviour of
complex IT systems. In addition, the deployment of AI (more speciﬁcally,
machine learning) technologies incorporating agents with the ability to adapt
and learn has implications for both agent-based simulation models and IT
systems in terms of the robustness of IT infrastructure, scalability, security
and adaptability.

Note: It is important to recognise that even some very simple systems
composed of only two components can generate non-linear behaviour.

3.2 Emergence
IT systems of ever increasing complexity are ﬁnding their way into all areas
of society including commerce, health care, government and education. One
of the principal challenges associated with building such systems is managing
the transition from systems comprised of many relatively isolated, small-scale
components to large-scale, massively interconnected systems that are
physically distributed yet which must remain secure, robust and efﬁcient.
Conventional approaches based on system decomposition (or ‘divide and
conquer’) are inappropriate since they cannot address this kind of problem,
geared as they are to the production of modular, hierarchical and centralised
forms of organisation. This has motivated consideration of alternative
decentralised approaches, inspired by natural systems such as animal brains,
immune systems and ant colonies, in which the desired system properties and
behaviours of the IT system are designed to emerge in a bottom-up fashion.

3.2.1 What is emergence?
Like complexity, the term emergence has somewhat different meanings in
different ﬁelds such as systems thinking, complexity science and philosophy.
According to systems thinker Peter Checkland (1981, p. 78), emergence
should be understood in terms of hierarchical levels of organisation, where
each level is ‘characterised by emergent properties which do not exist at the
lower level’. By contrast, more recent conceptions of emergence associated
with complexity theory emphasise component-level interaction as the means
by which system-level properties or behaviours are generated, with the latter
not reducible to the properties or behaviours of the components considered in
isolation. For example, the temperature and pressure of a gas can be viewed
as emerging from the collisions between large numbers of gas molecules:
temperature and pressure are properties that can be ascribed to the mass of
gas, but they do not exist as properties of isolated individual gas molecules.

In contrast to the top-down nature of simple and complicated systems that are
decomposable, centrally controlled and hierarchically organised, emergence in
complex systems is a bottom-up phenomenon in which the components
responsible for generating the aggregate properties or behaviours of the
system exist at one level, while the aggregate properties or behaviours
themselves exist at a higher level as shown in Figure 3.4.

is minimised while that of wanted behaviour is maximised, and this can
be attempted through incorporating positive and negative feedback loops
as appropriate.

5 Emergent structures, properties and behaviours in complex IT systems
tend to be irreversible and cumulative, so it is important to ensure that
only those that are ‘right’ for the system are generated.

In the next section, we will explore how the production of emergent
behaviours, properties and structures in complex IT systems can be inﬂuenced
or ‘steered’ in appropriate directions – that is, those promoting success and
avoiding failure.

3.3 Self-organisation and complex adaptive
systems (CAS)
In the previous section, we looked at the idea of emergence in relation to
complex systems and explored the relevance of this idea for building
successful complex IT systems through the control of emergence. In this
section, we build on this idea by examining the notions of self-organisation
and complex adaptive systems.

3.3.1 Self-organisation
The concept ofse lf-organisation refers to the ability of certain kinds of
systems to organise themselves – or rather, their components – into more
ordered forms without the involvement of a central organising agency,
whether internal or external to the system. You can think about this in terms
of the interaction between local and global dynamics described in the
previous section. It is the very essence of self-organisation that none of the
components, either individually or in small groups, can directly control, or
even shape, the evolution of the system as a whole. The impact of any
component, no matter how powerful, on the system is indirectly through its
local interactions only. In their interactions with each other, components co-
create the system’s evolution, yet none of them, individually or in small
groups, is organising the interaction, the self-organisation, across the system.

139

Part 3 – System success – complexity, emergence, adaptation, co-evolution

Self-organisation has for many years been perceived as occurring in physical
systems – for example, liquid crystal formation and the ﬂocking behaviour
discussed in section 3.2.2. The notion has been applied to organisational
contexts where, according to Mitleton-Kelly and Land (2004), it refers to:

The spontaneous coming together of a group to perform a task (or for some
other purpose); the group decides what to do, how and when to do it; and
no one outside the group directs those activities.

(Mitleton-Kelly and Land, 2004, p. 6)

One example of self-organisation in an IT systems context is the activities of
a software development team using an agile method, where members of the
team choose how best to accomplish their work, rather than being directed by
others outside the team. According to organisational development and change
management consultant, Diana Larsen:

When we say an Agile team is self-organizing, we mean that a group of
peers has assembled for the purpose of bringing a software development
project to completion using one or more of the Agile methodologies. The
team members share a goal and a common belief that their work is
interdependent and collaboration is the best way to accomplish their goal.
Empowered team members’ reduce their dependency on management as
they accept accountability, and the team structure places ownership and
control close to the core of the work. Rather than having a manager with
responsibility for planning, managing and controlling the work, the team
members share increasing responsibility for managing their own work and
also share responsibility for problem-solving and continuous improvement
of their work processes.

(Larsen, 2004, p. 1)

Other examples involving the social (people, organisation) components of
sociotechnical systems include the self-organisation of distribution and/or the
steering of supply systems over time (Abbasi and Varga, 2022), and decisions
about organisational innovation and the introduction of new working practices
and techniques, although the latter generally occurs in a ‘top-down’ fashion
steered by senior management. Examples focusing more on the technological
component of IT systems include the self-organising activities of P2P (peer-
to-peer) ﬁle sharing networks and the self-organisation of the internet, a
major facilitator of complex networked IT systems. More recently, Benbya,
Pachidi and Jarvenpaa (2021) point to AI ‘as a tool for self-organising the
deﬁnition and resolution of problems via information processing, introducing
questions concerning the way that AI redeﬁnes a ﬁrm’s organisational design
approaches and information processing capabilities’ (p. 289).

3.3.2 What is a complex adaptive system?
Complex adaptive systems (CAS) is the name given to complex systems
that are not only capable of generating emergent properties and behaviours
through self-organisation, but are also capable of adaptively self-organising;
modifying themselves in response to changing conditions in their
environment so as to maintain or enhance the properties and behaviours of

140

Part 3 – System success – complexity, emergence, adaptation, co-evolution

the system. Put simply, CAS are capable of learning to adapt to changes in
their circumstances, a feature which makes them highly robust to
perturbations or disturbances originating outside the system, either in the
environment or another system. According to Merali (2006):

It is the capacity for self-organisation and adaptation that confers robustness
upon organisational forms in dynamic environments. This resonates with
Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety.

(Merali, 2006, p. 222)

Note: Put simply, Ashby’s law states that a greater range of actions
available to a system allows for compensation of a greater range of
perturbations. In an IT system context this should be understood in
terms of the ability of the system to respond to its environment by
drawing on a diverse set of actions, thereby contributing to its
resilience.

CAS are ubiquitous in the natural world and human society. Consider the
following three examples:

. The mammalian brain, a massively interconnected system of billions of

neurons (or nerve cells) which is responsible for coordinating an animal’s
sensory inputs and motor outputs.

. A population of animals, each of which are attempting to survive long

enough to reproduce, evolving over a number of generations via processes
of random genetic variation and Darwinian natural selection so as to
optimise the population to its environment.

. Economic markets composed of large numbers of independent traders,

each of whom pursues individual self-interest while interacting with each
other in the buying and selling of commodities resulting in the emergence
of optimum and stable market prices. Housing markets, banking and
energy companies are other examples of social systems whose
components are also driven by the pursuit of self-interest and which
generate similar behavioural outcomes.

It can be argued that in each case there is no central control yet the system
self-organises into a stable and adaptive conﬁguration by learning to adapt to
perturbations in its environment.

As with emergence, the notion of a CAS is somewhat difﬁcult to explain in
abstract terms. Perhaps the best way of understanding this concept is by
exploring a phenomenon found in the natural world – ant colony foraging
behaviour.

3.3.3 Adaptation
One of the differences between aﬂ ock of birds and an ant colony simulation
is that in the former, the principles governing the behaviour of individual
birds are ﬁxed, that is, they do not change over time as a consequence of
interactions with other birds; by contrast, the behaviour of ants in the colony
changes over time as a consequence of their indirect interactions with other
ants via pheromone trails. This change in behaviour is not haphazard, but is
determined by structural changes in the environment which are introduced
incrementally, and which lead to incremental changes in ant colony behaviour
– a form of learning.

Another important difference between patterns arising from the ﬂocking of
birds and the global dynamics of an ant colony is that patterns associated
with the former tend to be highly brittle, that is, sensitive to perturbations. A
ﬂock of birds is a complex emergent system, however it is not a robust
complex system; by contrast, the ant colony is a complex adaptive system

capable of emergence which demonstrates robustness. According to Ladyman,
Lambert and Wiesner (2013):

The order in complex systems [of this kind] is said to be robust because,
being distributed and not centrally produced, it is stable under perturbations
of the system … A centrally controlled system on the other hand is
vulnerable to the malfunction of a few key components.

(Ladyman, Lambert and Wiesner, 2013, p. 39)

A system can maintain its order (or organisation) by making use of error
correction mechanisms involving negative feedback, or through the use of
some form of redundancy mechanism involving multiple instances of the
same component performing the same role in the system such that
functionality can be maintained in the event of component failure. However,
in a CAS, order (or organisation) is said to be maintained via the system
learning to adapt to changes in its environment. It follows from this that
when attempting to build successful complex IT systems, it is not enough to
simply recognise that the systems may generate emergent behaviour, some of
it possibly unwanted; in order to continue to function in a changing
environment, complex IT systems must also be capable of adapting ‘on the
ﬂy’ to changing circumstances and in a self-organising, decentralised manner.

Mechanisms of adaptation
Two kinds of adaptation can be distinguished: the ﬁrst occurs in an agent
during its lifetime; the second takes place within a population of agents
across a number of generations and is generally referred to as evolution. In
biology, the concept of continuous evolution is that the characteristics of
species change gradually over time in response to environmental competition.
The chief mechanism for this change is natural selection – the greater
likelihood that those organisms with successful traits will survive, breed and
pass on those traits to subsequent generations. Such phenomena can be seen
in IT systems: for example, if a particular piece of software has a user
interface that is especially unfriendly, or if it enforces ways of working that
are at odds with existing organisational culture, then it is likely that the
software will be replaced or modiﬁed. More broadly, particular software
products or even types of software will be successful according to their ﬁt
with the needs of their environments.

in Block 3.

exploration of a ‘space’ or ‘landscape’ of possibilities for system

‘optimum’ conﬁguration may be neither possible nor desirable; however, as

3.4 Co-evolution
In the previous section, we looked at the idea of complex adaptive systems
and explored its relevance to the building of successful complex IT systems
that are capable of adapting to changing requirements. In this section, we
build on this idea by examining the notion of co-evolution, an idea drawn
from the study of natural ecosystems.

3.4.1 What is co-evolution?
One way of formulating the notion of co-evolution is in terms of one domain
or entity changing in the context of one or more others; in short, reciprocal
inﬂuence leading to change in the co-evolving entities. A more concrete
illustration of this concept can be found in biology where elements in an
ecosystem inﬂuence each other through their interactions with each other and
with their environment. Merali (2006) describes co-evolution as follows:

Consider an ecosystem cohabited by a diversity of species. The
environment, each individual and each species will affect and be affected
by the actions of the other individuals and species. The ﬁtness or chances
of survival for each species will be related to its ability to adapt to the
environmental changes and, over time, selective pressures (resulting from
the interaction of the habitat and surviving cohabiting species) will lead to
the evolution of new traits in the various populations, changes in the habitat
and the emergence of new species. Co-adaptation and co-evolution in
dynamic environments can be viewed as important mechanisms for
sustainability of the ecosystem.

Merali, 2006, p. 221)

The notion of co-evolution, while reasonably straightforward to explain, is
perhaps best understood by yet another natural world phenomenon – the
interactions of predators and their prey. These types of interactions are
immediately relevant in an IT systems context where, for example, inter-
systemic co-evolution is taking place between a large and dominant
organisation – the ‘predator’ – and a much smaller business attempting to
compete – the ‘prey’. It should be clear that the underlying message
generalises to other contexts.

3.4.2 Co-evolution between IT systems
When considering the relevance of the concept of co-evolution to IT systems,
we can – and arguably, should – differentiate co-evolution of organisations
and their environments from co-evolution that takes place within
organisations. With respect to the former, Lewin, Long and Carroll (1999)
maintain that:

Strategic and organization adaptations coevolve with changes in the
environment (competitive dynamics, technological, and institutional) and
organization population and forms, [and] new organizational forms can
mutate and emerge from the existing population of organizations.

(Lewin, Long and Carroll, 1999, p. 535)

In this connection, Allen and Varga (2006) make a number of important
observations which are worth quoting in full. In their view, ‘if organizations

in the organization and outside it), then change is an outcome of interaction
in those co-evolving processes’ (p. 230). On this basis, they argue that IT
systems ‘are themselves complex systems, which display structural evolution
over time, and furthermore that they are themselves part of a larger complex
system of a business co-evolving with its environment’ (p. 236). Crucially,
they maintain that:

A complex system does not exist in isolation. It both competes and
collaborates with other organizations for resources and customers, at many
layers within the organization, for example, individuals, teams and
departments. Co-evolution occurs when the direct or indirect interaction of

(Allen and Varga, 2006, p. 230)

with in a chain or cascade of successive adjustments and adaptations based

also plays a role in the complex system of a wider economic and social
reality in which it is embedded. The changes reﬂect the feedback between a

3.4.3 Co-evolution within IT systems
Insofar as IT systems, being examples of sociotechnical systems, are
composed of technological components (IT) and the human components
associated with them (organisational structures and personnel), the sub-
systems within an IT system can – and arguably should – be viewed as co-
evolving systems. For this reason, there is a need to consider co-evolution
within the human and organisational sub-systems of an IT system itself.
Evolution operates at the ‘microscopic’ level; in organisations, this is the
individual or agent, and each agent has its own view of the organisation,
using personal constructs to cope with organisational life. These constructs
co-evolve through interactions with other agents which enables learning to
take place and construction of a shared, consensual view of the organisation
and IT system; this, in turn, impacts on each agent’s ability to evolve the IT
system further, an example of the interaction between local (or generating)
and global (or constraining) dynamics as described in section 3.2.2.

IT system alignment
Another area where the concept of co-evolution can be applied in an
organisational context is IT system alignment which concerns the attempt by

and plans support and are supported by the business mission, objectives, and

chronic unsolved problem in today’s complex and turbulent world.’ (p. 284)
In their view, IT system alignment should not be understood as an event, but
rather as a process of continuous adaptation and change, with both the system
as a whole and its constituent parts undergoing co-evolution.

3.5 Problems
Despite the importance of ideas such as complexity, emergence, adaptation
and co-evolution for understanding and dealing with large, massively
interconnected and networked IT systems, a number of challenges associated
with complex systems remain which, if solved, could contribute to promoting
the success and minimising the failure of complex IT systems. As Bullock
and Cliff (2004) observe:

Systems concepts such as complexity and emergent behaviour are
increasingly being recognised as important to meeting current urgent
engineering goals. However, as yet there is no established design

153

Part 3 – System success – complexity, emergence, adaptation, co-evolution

methodology capable of rigorously supporting their use in industrial-scale
ICT engineering.

(Bullock and Cliff, 2004, p. 5)

Examples of open problems in design methodology include:

. How to model coupling (that is, inter-relationships) between complex

systems and between them and their environments.

. How processes of growth and development constrain the functionality of

complex adaptive systems.

. How to avoid the brittleness exhibited by large engineered systems in

favour of a balance between robustness and ﬂexibility.

. How automatic mechanisms such as those associated with evolutionary
and adaptive computing – genetic algorithms and neural networks, for
example – can be used to conﬁgure or optimise the organisation of
complex systems.

Notwithstanding the above concerns, there are additional issues to consider in
connection with the application of ideas from complexity theory to large,
massively interconnected and networked IT systems.

3.5.1 Limits of formal and mathematical approaches
In its original form, complexity theory has a scientiﬁc and mathematical
character that is well supported by theoretical and empirical evidence, and the
concept of a complex system has increasingly been conceptualised in formal
terms as an information-theoretical entity. This has a number of implications
for complex IT systems since, as sociotechnical systems, the former are not
purely computational or informational in the sense of being composed from
exclusively technological components: as you learned in the ﬁrst part of this
module, while IT systems certainly contain IT components, they also contain
human components and formalisations of the latter are not readily
forthcoming. Despite the insights gathered from a range of disciplines
including management science, game theory, behavioural and operations
research and social psychology, attempts at constructing mathematical models
and simulations of social behaviour and decision making processes remain of
questionable validity and usefulness. As Mitleton-Kelly and Land (2004)
state:

Human activity systems … differ from all other complex evolving systems
in one important respect, in that the human actors are able to reﬂect and to
make intentional decisions.

(Mitleton-Kelly and Land, 2004, p. 2)

There is also the need to consider that IT systems might contain components
that simply cannot be formalised. As Mitleton-Kelly and Land go on to
argue:

Connectivity and interdependence may be part of the purposeful design of
the human activity systems. As such it may be explicit and reﬂected in
artefacts such as an organisation chart. But equally it may be informal, non-

154

Part 3 – System success – complexity, emergence, adaptation, co-evolution

articulated, tacit and be the result not of purposeful design but serendipitous
encounters.

(Mitleton-Kelly and Land, 2004, p. 3)

This view is shared by other IT system practitioners and theorists such as
Allen and Varga (2006) who maintain that:

It cannot be assumed that the interaction of the individuals will necessarily
lead to the successful functioning of the organization, or that there is any
scientiﬁc principle that ensures the functioning will be optimal, or indeed
even maintained. Instead, complexity science sees an organization that is
operating as being the result of a historical pathway of local
experimentation that has led to a system that works well enough to have
survived until now. Its operation affords no simple guarantee about future
survival.

(Allen and Varga, 2006, p. 230)

Accepting the irreducibly sociotechnical nature of IT systems, i.e. they are
foundationally not simply IT/technical, has a number of implications. Perhaps
the most important is that, in its application to social systems, especially
organisations, and, by extension, to sociotechnical systems, complexity theory
has generally tended to play a somewhat metaphorical role, providing the
guiding inspiration behind principles rather than fully ﬂedged implementation
mechanisms. Rather than using mathematics, application of complexity theory
in this context draws upon concepts that have been demonstrated
mathematically or experimentally in natural and artiﬁcial systems, and derives
lessons for the human components of IT systems which are examples of
sociotechnical systems. This is the approach we have adopted throughout this
Part.

3.5.2 Complexity and power relations
The presence of human intentional – that is, purposive or goal-directed –
decision making in sociotechnical systems such as complex IT systems is
signiﬁcant, since decision making within organisations requires factoring in
stakeholder interests and consideration of the power relations between them.
For example, in the context of discussing complex sociotechnical systems
Sussman (2010) rightly points out that:

Multiple stakeholders of complex, sociotechnical systems have their
disparate interests [and thus quite different goals and objectives] and hence
their different ways of evaluating system performance. We call this
evaluative complexity. Resolving evaluative complexity often creates ethical
dilemmas as, in effect, choices among stakeholders are made by the system
designers and operators.

(Sussman, 2010, pp. 6−7)

This position is supported by Mitleton-Kelly and Land (2004) who argue:

Complex systems are multidimensional, and all the dimensions interact and
inﬂuence each other. In a human context the social, cultural, technical,

economic, political and global dimensions may impinge upon and inﬂuence
each other.

(Mitleton-Kelly and Land, 2004, p. 3)

Note: In this module, we take a much more deﬁnite position than
Mitleton-Kelly and Land, arguing that such factors certainly do impact
upon each other and strongly.

Consistent with the above positions, and in the context of discussing the
complexity of networked systems, Merali (2006) maintains that:

The introduction of human agency, bounded rationality and free will adds to
the complexity of the network. It is impossible for any one agent in the
network to have complete knowledge of the state of the whole network at
any given time. Agents must act on the basis of the limited information that
they can glean from their network and immediate environment. The overall
state of the network emerges from the local actions of the individual agents,
none of whom have complete knowledge of the entire network, and all of
whom are susceptible to conditioning by their diverse social and cultural
environments and backgrounds, their personal experiences, and events and
information about events from their immediate environment and their
extended networks.

(Merali, 2006, p. 218)

Finally, Allen and Varga (2006) offer the following by way of advice:

Our understanding of reality is shaped by the IS we use. And our

3.6 Summary
In this Part, it has been argued that the appearance of networked IT systems
with increasingly large numbers of components that are massively
interconnected has prompted the need to look for ways of understanding and
dealing with the behaviour of such systems with a view to promoting system
success and minimising the possibility of failure. In this connection, four
concepts – complexity, emergence, adaptation and co-evolution – have been
explored, and a number of principles based on these concepts that can be
applied to complex IT systems have been presented.

As the ants explore their environment, they deposit trails of pheromones, that
is, chemical markers, which decay and diffuse over time. Ants returning to
the nest from a food source will lay trails that other ants can follow on their

the food source will always have the most recent pheromone deposits, they
will attract the most ant trafﬁc thereby reinforcing the amount of pheromone
deposited in a positive feedback loop. The net result is that, in the absence of
a centralised control mechanism, colonies of foraging ants quickly and
reliably self-organise to discover the shortest navigable path from the nest to
a food source. Importantly, if the path ceases to be navigable, the colony will
